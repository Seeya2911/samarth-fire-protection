A useful fire safety audit does more than count extinguishers. It checks whether the building’s installed protection measures, escape routes, people and maintenance records work together as a practical emergency system.

Start with the building and its use

Record the building’s occupancy, height, floor count, basements, high-risk rooms, public areas, special hazards and any recent change in use. Review the latest approved fire plans and approvals available to the owner or facility manager. A residential tower, school, hospital, office and industrial premises do not have identical risks or requirements.

1. Escape routes and means of egress

  • Check that staircases, corridors, exit doors and final discharge routes are not blocked or used for storage.
  • Check exit signs and emergency lighting for visible damage and apparent operation.
  • Confirm fire doors close properly and are not wedged open or obstructed.
  • Check that access to refuge areas and fire-fighting access routes is maintained where applicable.
  • Confirm evacuation instructions are visible and understandable to occupants.

2. Active fire-protection systems

Review the building’s installed systems rather than assuming every building has the same equipment. Where provided, inspect the visible condition and accessibility of hydrants, landing valves, hose reels, hose boxes, sprinkler controls, fire pumps, water storage, extinguishers, detectors, manual call points and alarm panels.

Check available maintenance records and fault logs. Operational tests should be planned and supervised by competent personnel using the approved procedure so testing does not create an unintended impairment or water release.

3. Alarm, detection and communications

Review panel status, recurring faults, disabled or isolated points, detector condition, manual call-point access and any connection to voice or other emergency communication systems. Ask for a documented process to report, investigate and close faults. A panel should not be left switched off simply to silence a trouble indication.

4. Housekeeping and ignition risks

  • Look for combustible storage in electrical rooms, staircases, corridors and pump rooms.
  • Check that electrical panels are accessible and obvious signs of overheating or damaged cables are escalated.
  • Review controls for hot work, temporary wiring, waste, gas cylinders and other hazards relevant to the premises.
  • Check that fire doors, shafts and service penetrations have not been compromised by later work.

5. People, plans and records

Confirm that emergency roles are assigned, staff know how to raise an alarm, evacuation routes and assembly points are known, and assistance arrangements exist for people who may need support. Review induction, drill and contractor briefing records. Make sure current emergency contacts and building plans are accessible to the people who need them.

6. Turn findings into a corrective-action plan

For every finding, document the location, evidence, risk level, immediate control, permanent action, owner and due date. Track high-risk items to closure and keep test reports, service records, photographs and retest evidence together. A report that lists defects but never verifies closure is not an effective maintenance process.

Standards and site-specific requirements

The audit criteria must be based on current applicable legislation, the building’s approved fire strategy, relevant standards, manufacturer instructions and local authority directions. The Maharashtra Fire & Emergency Services resource page provides access to the State’s published fire legislation and rules.

This checklist is an educational starting point, not a statutory audit format and not a replacement for assessment by qualified, appropriately authorised professionals.
